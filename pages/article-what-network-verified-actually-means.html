What we mean when we say a record is “verified”

A record only earns trust when what's physically there matches what the network reports. Here's how that check actually works.

On this page

Two different kinds of truth What “verified” should mean Why relying on one check breaks down What a verified record looks like

“Verified” gets used loosely in infrastructure software — a word stamped on a record to make it sound more solid than it is. Here's what it should actually require.

Two different kinds of truth

There's what the network reports — which devices are talking, right now — and there's what's physically installed in the rack. Those are two different questions, and a record that only answers one of them isn't telling you the whole truth.

“A record isn't verified because someone entered it. It's verified because two independent checks agree.”

What “network-verified” should actually mean

It should mean a device is confirmed physically present in a specific location, and the network independently reports it live at that same location — two signals agreeing, not one signal being assumed to cover the other.

Why relying on just one check breaks down

A network-only check misses anything dormant, unplugged, or quietly misrouted. A physical-only check misses anything that changed logically without a cable ever moving. Either check alone leaves a real gap.

In plain terms

It's like verifying someone's identity with just a name, versus a name and a photo ID that actually matches — one check alone is easy to get wrong.

What an actually verified record looks like

It pairs a photographed physical sweep with the live network state, timestamped, for every single entry — so “verified” means something specific instead of just sounding reassuring.
